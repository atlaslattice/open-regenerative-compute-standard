# Atlas Lattice / ORCS — Maui Water, Ocean Regeneration & East Maui Stream Restoration
**Research white paper v1.1 | 2026-10-15**
**Status: PROPOSED · NON-CANON · ZERO REALIZED CREDIT · NOT AN ENDORSED PUBLIC PROJECT**

## Executive summary
Evaluate three separately sited, proposed **5 million US gallons/day (MGD)** seawater regeneration campuses in **West Maui, South/Central Maui and North Shore Maui** (15 MGD total). Integrate potable desalination, remineralization and corrosion control; selectively recover brine minerals; intercept wastewater/stormwater nutrients, microplastics and contaminants at source; biosolar, batteries, energy recovery and AI-assisted optimization; and test zero-liquid-discharge (ZLD) or near-ZLD options. Include existing water supply, capital replacement, operations, distribution rehabilitation, Native Hawaiian public-trust rights, and East Maui stream restoration in the alternatives analysis. This is a research design, not a claim of feasibility, permits, project funding or actual savings.

## 1. The East Maui diversion issue: accurate geography and history
The dispute concerns stream diversions across East Maui, particularly the historic **Nāhiku, Keʻanae, Honomanū and Huelo** water-license areas, not simply municipal water supplied to the town of Hāna. Beginning in the plantation era, East Maui Irrigation ditches and tunnels carried water toward Central Maui sugar cultivation. The Hawaiian Commercial & Sugar plantation ceased operations in 2016; successor land and irrigation arrangements involve **Mahi Pono** and East Maui Irrigation. Mahi Pono took full ownership of East Maui Irrigation in 2025. Central Maui agricultural water uses today should not be simplistically described as ongoing sugar monoculture, nor should all agriculture be characterized as inherently wasteful. Crop-specific water demand, irrigation losses, public benefit, and stream-flow impacts must be measured.

Native Hawaiian kalo farmers, Nā Moku Aupuni O Koʻolau Hui and allied organizations have opposed diversions they say impair stream ecosystems, loʻi kalo, and traditional and customary rights. Their opposition must be attributed accurately, and local communities must have genuine authority in restoration design rather than be presented as endorsing this hypothetical infrastructure project.

On July 21, 2026, Native Hawaiian Legal Corporation reported an Environmental Court reversal and a required contested-case hearing concerning the 2026 permit authorizing up to **41.72 MGD**; it said that permitted quantity was nearly twice 2025 actual diversions. The permit quantity is NOT actual flow or guaranteed restorable flow. On August 14, 2026, BLNR voted to advance the court-ordered hearing and rejected the long-term license proposal before it. October 8 reporting confirms the proceedings remain important and unresolved. Hawaiʻi's public-trust framework and instream flow standards govern any future changes.

## 2. Reference design, capacity and conservation
Per campus: 5 MGD finished potable water; at illustrative 45% RO recovery, seawater intake ~11.11 MGD and concentrate ~6.11 MGD. Across three campuses: **15 MGD** finished water, **33.33 MGD** intake, **18.33 MGD** concentrate, subject to actual chemistry, pretreatment, production uptime and reuse. Annual at 100% utilization: 5.475 billion gallons potable; ~46.1 million cubic meters feed and ~25.3 million cubic meters brine. Dissolved salts in feed at assumed 35 g/L: ~1.61 million tonnes/year, a feed inventory, NOT recovered product.

Do not presume all 15 MGD is needed, deliverable, or displaces East Maui diversion. Map source-to-customer transmission, elevations, reservoirs, redundancy and demand by service area. Design alternatives include (A) existing-system rehabilitation only, (B) phased 5-MGD campuses, (C) three-campus full buildout, and (D) hybrids with wastewater reuse and agricultural irrigation upgrades.

## 3. Existing infrastructure displacement ledger
For each legacy treatment plant, source well, diversion, pump, pipeline and reservoir, identify metered flow, reliability, source-water quality, chemical and power expense, staff and maintenance, upcoming capital replacement, debt service, and standby value. Distinguish **avoidable** operating expense and deferred capex from sunk costs, retained reservoirs, water-right obligations and watershed stewardship. No blanket assertion that Maui's compliant municipal drinking water is contaminated. Maui's county-published 2025 water-quality reports describe regulatory compliance, while localized fire-damaged-system remediation and aging-pipe concerns require separate, location-specific treatment.

The county's 2024 Water Shortage and Conservation Plan includes distinct Hāna, Central, Upcountry and Lahaina systems; do not pool them as one hydraulically interchangeable grid.

## 4. Stream restoration scenario ledger
The three-campus 15-MGD design capacity is **35.95% of the 41.72-MGD permitted diversion quantity** (15/41.72). This is ONLY a scale comparison; it is not an estimate of streamflow restored, because permitted diversions exceed reported 2025 actual use and the water may serve different customers. Illustrative, not forecast, cases:
- 0 MGD verified diversion offset: no stream-restoration credit.
- 2 MGD verified offset: 730 million gallons/year restored if 365-day equivalent flow, subject to ecological, seasonal and legal constraints.
- 5 MGD: 1.825 billion gallons/year.
- 10 MGD: 3.650 billion gallons/year.
Each case requires direct measured evidence that new supplies or irrigation efficiency reduce specific diversion volumes, and CWRM-approved instream protections. Evaluate summer low-flow and biological response, not only annual gallons. Distinguish municipal from agricultural offtake; price transmission and irrigation retrofits; protect kalo farming and customary gathering rights. Do not assign Native Hawaiian community consent or ownership without engagement.

## 5. Ocean pollution, ZLD and reef performance
Design zero **untreated** pollutant discharge as an enforceable performance principle and **true ZLD** as an engineering target tested against energy and lifecycle impacts. Brine is not automatically pollution removed from the ocean: it is concentrated seawater, and solid mineral residues still require handling. True ZLD includes crystallization and residual moisture accounting; it does not eliminate intake entrainment or other ecological impacts.

Three-campus indicative RO energy: 5.475 billion US gallons ~20.72 million m³ freshwater/year × 3.5 kWh/m³ = **72.5 GWh/year**. Brine ~25.3 million m³/year × illustrative incremental ZLD 10 kWh/m³ = **253 GWh/year**. Combined ~326 GWh/year, **$65.2M/year at $0.20/kWh**, excluding other expenses. These energy inputs are assumptions, not vendor quotes. Compare energy recovery, high-recovery membrane concentration, selective precipitation, crystallizers, renewable power and waste heat; avoid double counting recovered pressure energy.

Intercept wastewater nutrients, plastics and contaminants before coastal discharge. Design monitored stormwater capture, tertiary wastewater treatment, appropriate adsorbents, microplastic filters and independent laboratory testing. Measure microplastics by validated particle-size distribution and polymer type, captured mass, ultimate residue destination, nutrients, enterococci, beach advisories, coral cover, pH, total alkalinity, DIC and dissolved oxygen. Do not claim numerical reef or swim-safety benefits without site-specific baseline and models.

## 6. Minerals, magnesium, construction and public health
At the illustrative three-campus feed volume, seawater magnesium inventory is ~62,175 tonnes/year, corresponding to a **theoretical** ~149,000 tonnes/year Mg(OH)₂ product at perfect stoichiometric recovery. It is not a realistic commercial yield. Distinguish magnesium product for industrial sale, carefully controlled potable-water remineralization and experimental ocean alkalinity enhancement; do not double count one tonne. Potable mineral addition must use food/drinking-water-grade materials, validated water chemistry and corrosion controls. Evidence for special health benefits from drinking high-pH water is limited; taste and mineral balance are testable.

Ocean alkalinity enhancement requires complete acid/base, carbon, ecological and permitting accounting. A process that returns its acid co-product to the sea may erase the net benefit. No uncontrolled reef dosing.

Surfbrick/mortar research may use clean mineral precipitates or qualified residues, but sludge, captured microplastics and contaminated solids cannot be presumed safe. Require leachability, salts, strength, durability and marine-exposure tests; otherwise route to appropriate controlled disposal.

## 7. Biosolar, batteries and controls
Screen campus roofs, carports and previously disturbed land before new coastal land conversion. Example 20 PV-suitable acres per campus × 0.18 MWdc/acre × 3 = 10.8 MWdc; at 1,600 kWh/kWdc/year yields **17.28 GWh/year**, ~5.3% of the reference 326 GWh/year load, before losses and hourly mismatch. Batteries supply resilience and time-shifting, not net energy creation. Analyze pumped/gravity energy recovery where available; high-pressure RO pressure exchangers are the priority and must not be counted twice. Industrial AI supports leak detection, demand forecasts, membrane fouling and scheduling under hard certified safety controls; public water-quality data must be independently verified.

## 8. Economic accounting
Net social lifecycle cost = annualized new capex + new OPEX + retained legacy OPEX + transmission and decommissioning + environmental costs - verifiable avoided legacy OPEX - avoided future capex - independently saleable recovered product net margin - verifiable other attributable benefits. Avoid counting water tariffs as avoided production costs or simultaneously selling and consuming the same output. Compare Dongjiakou and Harbor Island only after aligning capacity, historical prices, construction scope, permitting, energy and intake/outfall. Mineral gross revenue is not net profit. Include Maui construction freight and environmental review.

**Bottom line:** Evaluate three ocean-resource campuses against the **full cost and water rights of the existing system**. Stream restoration is a potential benefit only where reductions in specific East Maui diversions can be demonstrated and protected in law. Water security, coastal ecology and traditional rights must be evaluated together rather than traded away invisibly.
