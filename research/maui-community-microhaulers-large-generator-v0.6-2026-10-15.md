## Executive concept

A distributed circular economy is fundamentally **a community service and jobs network**, not only a capital-intensive recycling facility. Test a model in which residents and small businesses receive scheduled presorted pickup by appropriately insured, trained local truck owners/operators, who make short-haul trips to **West Maui, Central Maui, North Shore and Upcountry/Kula** hubs. Subdivided regional pickup reduces dependence on difficult cross-island roads and creates opportunities for local independent businesses and worker cooperatives; heavier resort/commercial loads use suitable dedicated collection vehicles and compactors.

Treat the resident's observation of abundant trucks as a **local recruitment hypothesis**, NOT a demographic assertion that all Native Hawaiians, residents or Maui households own trucks or want waste-hauling work. Localized pay, eligibility, turnout and worker protection need surveys.

A parallel, community-reviewed **large waste generator diversion standard** could ask resorts, hotels, golf courses and other high-volume facilities to sort, measure and demonstrably process waste. It should apply by measured waste quantity, not tourists' wealth, and permit competitive service providers and qualified on-site processing. Never imply such a new requirement is already enacted.

## 1. Verified legal and policy baseline — distinguish what ALREADY exists

1. **Hawaiʻi Act 173, SLH 2026** (SB 2101 SD1 HD1 CD1; approved June 26, 2026) amends HRS §§342G-3 and 342G-26. The **statewide goals** for organic waste disposal via landfill/incineration/waste-to-energy are **40% reduction by 2038, 70% by 2043, 100% by 2048**. Counties must identify and assess methods to meet organic-waste goals, including invasive-species transport risk. The final Act **does NOT impose a direct resort diversion mandate** or enact the hotel-by-2027 threshold that was present in an earlier bill draft. This distinction is crucial.
2. **Maui County Code Chapter 20.22** already mandates **glass-container recycling for covered licensed on-site liquor-serving establishments**, with registration, recycling records and a mechanism for suspension when pickup/dropoff is unavailable. This is a narrow precedent for a broader rule, NOT proof one currently exists for all resort waste.
3. Maui's **2026 Water Use Conservation Ordinance 5929** includes lodging-industry efficiency practices and rules around new facilities and water reuse. A proposed circular-resource standard could complement it, but does not follow automatically from it.
4. **HRS §271-5(9)** exempts transportation of **garbage or refuse** from the ordinary PUC motor-carrier law. The PUC confirms refuse haulers are among exempt carrier classes; whether clean segregated reusable material hauled for compensation also qualifies depends on legal classification and should be confirmed. The exemption does not waive county commercial landfill accounts, DOH handling requirements, business licenses, insurance, weights, wages or safety.
5. **HRS §291C-131** requires waste-carrying vehicles to prevent escape/spillage and cover loose paper, rubbish and plastics. Open pickup truck beds are viable **only with secure sealed containers/tarpaulins, appropriate payloads and controls for odor, leachate, insects, wildfire ash, and road litter**.
6. **HRS §383-6** has state employee/independent contractor rules for unemployment coverage (commonly described as an ABC test); the federal FLSA also requires actual classification under applicable standards. Structuring a worker as a subcontractor on an app does not dispense with applicable workplace duties. **Offer genuine operator co-ops, payroll employment, or properly classified independent businesses**.

## 2. Who does what — four-tier regional service architecture

| Tier | Local operating mode | Eligible waste | Destination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resident optional subscriber | Local truck owner/employee cooperative, 1–2 scheduled visits weekly | Source-separated sealed food scraps and sorted dry items in independent leakproof bins; contamination-screened | Nearest authorized West, Central, North or Kula aggregation site |
| Local shops and smaller businesses | Denser scheduled commercial routes, documented volume and fees | More paper, cardboard, food scraps, glass | Regional hubs; existing compliant processors |
| Large resorts, hotels and golf courses | Compactor/roll-off or purpose-built collection with contracted weights, regular inspections; no assumption a pickup bed can safely haul large volumes | High-volume organics, recyclable glass/metals/plastics, landscaping, wastewater-derived nutrients where compliant | Suitable licensed processor or verified onsite certified systems |
| Specialized waste | Certified licensed processors | Batteries/EVs, oils, refrigerants, asbestos, lead paints, ash, biohazards, e-waste | Approved handling and custody — NO mixed municipal/3D-print feedstock |

Regional hubs may receive and aggregate clean materials, but **landfill-access rules and DOH facility permits apply**; a residential self-haul privilege cannot be converted into free commercial disposal.

**Ocean-first controls:** no loose waste lost during transport; storm-rated covered hubs, biosecure compost and digestate operations, pest/vector control, stormwater microplastic screens, route cleanup, a sealed collection chain, and audited destination weights. Land and beach cleanup outcomes are separately measured.

## 3. Route-level constraints, costs and employment

**Reference v0.5 subscription mix (all purely assumed):**
- **4,200** residences × **52** stops/year = **218,400** annual household pickups.
- **380** small commercial properties × **104** = **39,520** commercial pickups.
- **23** large resorts/properties × **156** = **3,588** large commercial pickups.
- Material weights assumed: resident **9 kg**, small business **75 kg**, resort **350 kg** per visit.
- **Total mass ~6,185.4 metric tonnes/year (~6,818 U.S. short tons)**. These are hypothetical inputs; **not collected waste or signed supply**.

### 3.1 Household stop cost alternatives

The v0.5 user-supplied baseline used **$20 per stop** direct cost. v0.6 tests **$14 all-in per residential visit**, ONLY if route density allows:

| $14 component | $/residential stop | Annual total at 218,400 visits |
|---|---:|---:|
| Pay for local operator labor, gross before personal taxes | $8 | $1,747,200 |
| Reimbursed/contracted truck energy, maintenance, commercial insurance | $4 | $873,600 |
| Local aggregation, scan, weigh and depot receiving | $2 | $436,800 |
| **Direct route + receiving total** | **$14** | **$3,057,600** |

This is **not a Hawaii wage survey or vendor quote**. The $8/stop cannot be treated as take-home pay after business overhead unless the latter is reimbursed as assumed. Insurance and payroll burden may require higher figures, and $4/stop vehicle allowance may or may not cover fuel, wear, insurance and empty returns.

At eight all-in stops/hour, households alone account for **27,300 route hours/year**, or **~15.2 full-time-equivalent positions at 1,800 hours/year**. With 5–12 stops/hour, ~**24.3 to 10.1 FTE**, **not** 10–24 guaranteed unique jobs. Include additional hub/bioreactor/QA staff as separate labor-budgeted roles only when staffing plans exist. Service may be split among more part-time operators.

At 8 household stops/hour, 5 hours/day implies **40 household pickups per route day** and, at 9 kg/stop, **360 kg material** before bins/tools; eligibility depends on each truck's manufacturer payload, actual weights and sealed bed construction. Counting 40 stops does not prove one truck can do the route. Model loading, transfer, queuing, beach/highway time, wastewater leakage risk, weekend access, cargo volume and storm closures. At island reference 218,400/year and 260 working days, **840 household pickups/day**, or **~21 pickup-equivalent shifts/day** at 40 pickups/shift.

Operating design: recruit qualified operators, offer verified hourly earnings floor (subject to employment status), monthly safety audits, non-discriminatory intake, culturally appropriate training, predictable routes, public pricing, optional local franchise/cooperative shares, and insurance/vehicle/maintenance support.

### 3.2 Route benefit vs prior baseline

At unchanged rates and 218,400 household pickups/year, reducing direct household cost from $20 to $14 (actual verified) saves **$1,310,400/year**; this is a sensitivity result only. If pickup and depot costs actually total $20, the financial advantage vanishes. The larger-scale benefit may still be better community access/resilience/jobs if substantiated.

**Before processing and plant capital:**

| Pricing/cost scenario | Annual route net contribution after $800,000 shared overhead |
|---|---:|
| v0.5: residential direct cost $20/stop | **$441,760** |
| v0.6: residential direct cost $14/stop | **$1,752,160** |

All else assumed: resident **$15**/pickup; small business **$75 fee/$45 cost** twice weekly; large commercial/resort **$500 fee/$180 cost** three times weekly. **Resort participation and price are speculative.** Commercial route pricing should scale with weighed mass/volume and required service, with competitive compliant providers, not a fixed assumption that every operator is wealthy.

## 4. Resort and high-volume generator responsibility: two lawful policy alternatives

**Policy A — voluntary contracted service with incentives**
Resorts meet measured recycling/organics benchmarks through self-processing, qualified vendors or networks. Contracts may obtain waste-hauling cost relief, publicly audited sustainability reporting, construction/landscape-product offtake and differentiated service prices. Use as near-term commercial business case without assuming regulation.

**Policy B — prospective large-generator circular-waste standard (requires county/state legal review and process)**
Coverage by **verified annual tonnage, stream type and available treatment capacity**, e.g. an illustrative tier for >1 U.S. short ton per week of food/green waste; actual threshold subject to waste composition survey and stakeholder/county decisions. Require source separation, weigh-ticket tracking, an approved route or legal onsite reuse/compost, glass compliance, reduction plans, contamination control and annual public outcome reporting. Large venues, food markets, resorts and institutional kitchens could all qualify by the SAME measured generation criterion. Smaller and low-income generators receive grants/technical assistance, phased timing and reasonable exceptions for lack of services or emergencies.

Do **not** lock businesses into Atlas as the sole provider, retroactively charge absent a law, add unapproved hotel taxes, or assume county power to compel particular suppliers. Review due process, state preemption, disposal law, workforce rights, land use and contracting before any proposal. A requirement for responsibly managed source-separated waste does **not** require every resort to install an onsite bioreactor if aggregate regional systems outperform it.

**Critical economic principle:** Under Policy B a service program could gain predictable volume, but a legal mandate is **not revenue** until prices/participation are contractually established. Compare economic effects on small businesses, resorts, workers and residents; report all projected costs.

## 5. Updated 5–10-year payback sensitivity

Assume default v0.6 optimistic dense-route $14 household pickup cost and same business/resort contracts/prices as v0.5; no grants or signed customers; all intake **6,818.24 US short tons/year** eligible for **net** material contribution after every material-processor expense and rejects. This eligibility is optimistic: reality may be lower.

- Collections after shared overhead: **$1,752,160/year**.
- Illustrative net materials contribution **$200 per US short ton**: **$1,363,647/year**.
- Combined annual steady-state cash before capital, tax and financing: **~$3,115,807/year**.

| Assumed initial gross CAPEX | Simple payback at $200 net/t |
|---:|---:|
| $15M | **4.8 years** |
| $25M | **8.0 years** |
| $35M | **11.2 years** |

For a **$25M** system, an unsubsidized 10-year simple payback at these collection assumptions needs ~**$110 net processing contribution per eligible U.S. short ton**; a five-year payback requires ~**$476/t**. If customer acceptance, material values or route density underperform, the payback fails. These are **simple** (not discounted) paybacks and assume full utilization from Year 1, which is unlikely. All site acquisition, permits, staffing, new facilities, insurance, energy, financing, and process residual costs must be adequately included in CAPEX/annual expense/net-processing-margin before these figures represent business economics.

**Sensitivity:**
- Household true collection cost returns to $20/stop: subtract **$1.3104M/year**.
- All 23 large accounts pay $300 instead of $500 per pickup: subtract **$717,600/year**.
- Unqualified feedstock/reject fraction 30%: multiply net material contribution by **0.70**, holding same collection route costs.
- Additional annual bioreactor/yard cost $500k not included in quoted net processor margin: subtract **$500k/year**.
- No large accounts enroll: remove their net route contribution (**$1.148M/year**) and their *non-overlapping* eligible material weight.

Use the companion calculator's control inputs to model each downside case. A claim of five-to-ten-year repayment is **not verified**; prioritize corrected metrics, worker conditions and quality of life over forecast certainty.

## 6. Network jobs and circular off-take

**Resident service first:** affordable community recycling fees, pickup reliability, benefits from avoidable waste and clean streets. Community benefit agreements for local contracts and worker co-op equity are proposals requiring member approval.

**Materials:** 3D-printed code-tested nonstructural components and future certified housing; Surf Block ICCF supply; high-quality plastics and metal reuse; recycled glass and safe aggregate; water/wastewater nutrient recovery; measured fertilizer displacement. Service routes are NOT allowed to dump contaminated plastics into compost that returns to fields.

**Tourism/hospitality demand:** large properties can contract to purchase local soil amendments, landscape products and permitted recycled materials where standards and prices are competitive. Contracting is distinct from mandatory sourcing.

**Ocean-first:** preserve shoreline habitat, upstream trash and microplastic removal, marine-safe transport and processing, no waste-leachate releases, independent baseline sampling. Nearshore alkalinity modification remains a research-only gated concept, not an operational pickup service.

**Interisland exports:** only if local Maui demand and reserve goals are met, product quality certified and freight cost included; no guaranteed high margin from geographic proximity.

## 7. Next verifiable research and implementation gates

1. **Field route trial:** at least two actual dense and two dispersed Maui residential routes with measured stops/hour, payloads, insured vehicle costs, service duration, driver pay and transfer hours. Include Honoapiʻilani Highway road-closure scenarios and wet-weather conditions.
2. **Commercial waste audits:** 5–10 resorts/hotels, 5 grocery/institutional kitchens and 10 smaller businesses, anonymized actual sorted weights, current hauling bills, shift hours, participation.
3. **No government funds presumed:** grants only when eligible and committed to this entity; $3.607M EPA West Maui collection-center award is a public third-party project and not private business cash.
4. **Worker and facility legal check:** waste-law exemptions scope, insurance, worker classification, PUC interpretation of clean commodities, county landfill accounts, applicable recycling and composting permits, landowner approvals and truck load protection.
5. **Capacity first, mandate second:** any large-generator standard must include evidence of available permitted processing capacity, practical compliance pathways, transparency, due process and public comment.
6. **Measure benefits separately:** in-island wages actually paid; business utility/material savings actually invoiced; tonnes diverted; rural access; runoff microplastic samples; farm soil fertility; local jobs, resilience and quality of life.
7. **Preserve versioned receipts and audit trails** in GitHub; no realized credits until externally confirmed.

**Conclusion:** local pickup-truck micro-enterprises and audited large-waste-generator standards are a viable design hypothesis with promising legal precedents. A testable network, fair labor, real collection infrastructure, and actual material sales determine whether it becomes a profitable, locally beneficial Maui circular economy rather than another attractive paper plan.
