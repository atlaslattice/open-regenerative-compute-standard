# Maui Axis Deer: In-Place Paddock Containment, Welfare-First Stewardship, and Venison Food Security
**Atlas Lattice / ORCS · Research White Paper v0.2 · October 16, 2026**

**Status:** PROPOSED RESEARCH · NON-CANON · NO SITE SELECTED · NO CAPTURE APPROVED · NO MOU OR MAUI NUI PARTNERSHIP · NO STATE INCENTIVE RECEIVED · ZERO REALIZED CREDIT

## Executive abstract
Explore whether selected cooperating Maui landowners could convert **existing axis-deer activity sites** into licensed, veterinary-supervised, ecologically accountable **contained pasture systems**. The project aims to reduce free-ranging damage, preserve social groups, evaluate animal welfare against existing professionally inspected wild harvest, and develop an additional local protein option. This is **not** an endorsement of capturing all Maui deer, increasing invasive deer breeding, or assuming captive life is always more humane. A net reduction in free-ranging deer and no escapes are essential gates.

**Most important discovery:** Australian national feral-deer management guidance explicitly documents *paddock traps*—large enclosures offering feed, water and cover, with remotely operated or other managed entry systems—and has recorded corral trapping of chital/axis deer. This supports the **capture concept**, not lifetime captive production. A 2017 chital trapping experiment documented both deer habituation and significant fence/behavior and welfare difficulties. Sources: https://pestsmart.org.au/toolkit-resource/trapping-of-feral-and-wild-deer/ and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321403198_Final_Report_Chital_Deer_Trapping .

## 2. Existing traps: what they actually do
**Corral trapping:** temporarily contains a group within a dedicated capture pen. Gate closure may be remotely controlled; professional procedures include minimizing disturbance and injury. It is **not** the same as a captive deer farm.

**Paddock trapping:** paddock-scale pasture enclosure with water, feed, cover and suitable controlled entries. The Australian NATSOP-DEER003 mentions paddock traps; larger spaces may allow deer to move away from people and reduce fence pressure in capture. **Not proof of long-term containment success.**

**Single-animal Clover-style traps:** suited to particular research/management circumstances but not obviously suitable for retaining an entire established herd.

**Darting / chemical immobilization:** requires veterinarians; may increase stress, capture myopathy, mortality, drug-residue restrictions and handling complexity. Not necessarily safer than an enclosure approach.

**Research distinction:** An in-place transition from voluntary pasture use to lifetime, monitored husbandry is a new intervention. The existing trap research mostly concerns animal control and immediate removal. It does not establish stocking rate, long-term welfare, breeding optimization, operating cost, licensing or meat quality.

**No deployment recipe:** Any trap operation requires professional design, landowner and agency authorization, trained animal-welfare personnel, species-specific fencing and emergency escape/rescue safeguards.

## 3. Three operating alternatives

A. **Status quo enhanced wild harvest.** Work with inspected Maui Nui-like existing suppliers and landowners; potentially expand local food distribution while continuing ecological removal. Avoid duplicate new processing infrastructure.

B. **In-place capture-and-finish (nonbreeding).** On authorized private ranchland, evaluate small proof-of-concept capture, veterinary oversight, monitored pasture use, and later legally inspected food production without expanding the captive breeding stock. Requires evidence that extra feed doesn't attract further invasive animals or displace cattle forage.

C. **Managed breeding herd (conditional).** Retain a small defined breeding group only if proven conservation- and welfare-compatible. Calibrate female age, age at first birth, gestation, fawn survival, replacement breeding stock, growth and finishing using *local measured records*. One captive-born generation may be easier to habituate; genetic domestication and parasite-free status are not conferred by birth in captivity.

Option B is the earliest viable pilot candidate **to evaluate**, while A is a proven benchmark. Option C remains speculative.

## 4. Animal welfare and parasite safety
Use Five Domains-style independent oversight: suitable nutrition/water, physical environment, health, behavior and mental state. A familiar territory with reliable feed may still become unsafe if confinement causes panicked collision, overcrowding or abnormal social conflict. The 2017 chital experiment emphasized translocation risks and capture myopathy. Do not equate “no tranquilizer” with “no distress.”

Protected / non-harvest cohort requires documented lifetime funding and veterinary triage; sick/injured animals may need treatment or humane euthanasia rather than automatic indefinite mixing with healthy stock. Parasites, coccidia, bacterial contamination, potential shared cattle infections, and food-borne pathogens remain concerns across generations. Quarantine and veterinary diagnostics are site-specific.

Potential cattle co-grazing is **unverified**. Start with separate management; test shared disease risks, forage competition, water and grazing rotations. The pasture carrying capacity is unknown until evaluated through measured forage productivity and ecological constraints.

## 5. Population conservation and scaling
**26,000 deer** is an upper planning population, not a capturable inventory. Each landowner site requires independent counts of resident animals, legally available acreage, habitat constraints, birth rate, deaths, crossings and ecological damage.

Existing screened operator benchmark at **29.59–42.50 lb food/deer**:
- 1,000 deer annual harvest → approximately **29,590–42,500 lb packaged food** (conditional on actual age, weights, inspection pass and sales mix).
- 100 deer annual harvest → approximately **2,959–4,250 lb**.
- Hypothetical 26,000 resident deer at 15–30% annual harvest → **3,900–7,800 heads/year**, roughly **115k–332k lb food/year** depending on operator year yield. **NOT** a defensible sustainable rate absent captive-husbandry demographic data.

**No perpetual full-population annual harvest:** keeping a breeding population alive means first-year total harvest cannot be replicated annually. Capture/inventory, harvest, breeding-stock replacement, excluded animals and conservation constraints must reconcile every head and every birth/death.

## 6. Financial ledger and 30% margin hurdle
Economic segments must stay separate:
- **Ranch/paddock business:** site lease opportunity cost, permitted capture, setup and annual fence care, pasture and water, supplementation, veterinary handling, herd observation, reproduction management and welfare-committed animal care.
- **Inspection/cold chain:** authorized harvest/slaughter, refrigerated handling, product specification, processing, packaging, insurance, licensing, transport and sales channel.
- **Product/restaurant:** raw venison input, mycoprotein ingredient, heme ingredient, bun, energy, packaging, labor and customer price. Do not confuse $14 prepared cheeseburger retail price with raw venison wholesale revenue.
- **Public incentives / landscape:** official qualifying $50-tails awards only if approved and actually collected; the default **$0 for captive live transfers**. Demonstrated avoided damage is separate from food-sales gross margin until owner accounts prove marginal savings.

**Earlier v0.2 scenario caveats:** A 30%-margin illustration at ~$25/lb received by operator, 30% head harvest, $100/head/year animal care, $4/lb processing and $15k/year fixed site cost is an **optimistic unverified sensitivity case**, *not* a quote or forecast. Land is owned does not imply pasture opportunity cost = zero; site-specific carrying capacity and fence CapEx/depreciation can dominate. At $12/lb wholesale, those assumptions did not achieve the 30% goal even when herd size increased. A separate island feedstock/welfare sanctuary fund may be required.

**Margin definition must be explicit:** operating profit/revenue, with revenues recognized only at actual signed realized price; record labor, maintenance, amortization/depreciation, buyer margin, shrink and compliance.

## 7. Local food product diversification
**Existing:** inspected wild axis-deer venison is demonstrated on Maui. Value-add opportunities include ground venison, sausage, jerky/charcuterie under appropriate food rules, restaurant and local household packs; avoided imports only when island consumption displaces proven baseline purchased non-island protein.

**Experimental hybrid:** venison + certified food-grade mycoprotein may stretch a scarce red-meat supply and change fat/protein/texture. Test recipe mass balance, sensory acceptance, true ingredient costs and labeling under FSIS/FDA rules. It is **not vegetarian or vegan** when venison is present.

**Animal-free analogue:** wholly separate formulation with food-grade fungal protein and legally qualified precision-fermented bovine myoglobin; FDA GRN 1001 has a 2021 no-questions response for specified flavor/aroma uses up to 2% in ground-meat analogues. Do not assume availability or manufacturer IP rights. https://hfpappexternal.fda.gov/scripts/fdcc/index.cfm?id=1001&set=GRASNotices

**Waste-to-food caution:** slaughter blood cannot be scooped from a waste bin and declared edible; only lawfully inspected hygienic human-food collection and processing. Supplier availability, condemned portion and food-grade coproducts are UNKNOWN.

## 9. Go/no-go pilot metrics
**G0 legal/ecological:** DLNR/DOFAW landowner and wildlife approvals, veterinary authority, permits, statutory deer transport and game restrictions, and no conflict with conservation plans; stop absent sign-off.
**G1 habitat:** verified landowner, water, forage carrying capacity, contamination history, protected/native habitat, secure boundaries and escape response.
**G2 welfare:** measured pre/post stress, mortality/injuries, flight behavior, calf/fawn nursing continuity, disease prevalence and independent review against inspected wild-harvest baseline.
**G3 food:** approved slaughter/inspection/cold chain; accepted lb/head and ingredient traceability, no unsafe carcasses in commerce.
**G4 economics:** site actuals for fence/care, processing, land opportunity cost, risk reserves; ≥30% operating margin only if actually documented including all relevant costs, not inferred from retail price.
**G5 conservation:** monitored decline in unmanaged deer impact and no growth/escape of invasive stock. Population and ecological benefit counted separately from business receipts.

## Conclusion
Maui may benefit from evaluating a **distributed in-place paddock-capture and limited husbandry approach** on cooperating landowner properties as a humane alternative *in some circumstances*, complementary to existing certified wild-harvest systems. Evidence supports that deer corral/paddock capture methods exist, local deer food processing operates at scale, and state landowner-control programs exist. Evidence **does not yet support** lifetime captive-farming welfare superiority, 30% profit, permissibility of a new farm, official trap purchases of a stated design, 26k deer capturability, assured inspection approval, renewable annual harvest fraction, or eligibility for $50 payments on enclosed living animals.

**Next research action:** request a joint evidence review from DOFAW, veterinarians, Maui Nui, ranch landowners and USDA FSIS before designing infrastructure. Preserve all assumptions as versioned non-canon evidence.

# Appendix A — Maui Venison Palatability & Value-Added Mini Cookbook
**v0.2 addition · October 16, 2026 · EXPERIMENTAL, NOT A VALIDATED COMMERCIAL RECIPE OR NOVELTY CLAIM**

## A1. Evidence update: venison and three marinade families

**Confirmed prior art, not an invention:** peer-reviewed studies directly investigated pineapple-juice and kefir treatments on deer muscle, and shio-koji, miso, ginger, pineapple and kiwi treatments on deer meat. Venison al pastor recipes also exist in the public culinary domain. The Maui application is about measured flavor/texture optimization, qualified island ingredients and food-chain efficiency—not claiming to invent pineapple tenderization or al pastor.

**2012 original experiment:** Żochowska-Kujawska, Lachowicz & Sobczak, *Meat Science* 92(4), 675–680, “Effects of fibre type and kefir, wine lemon, and pineapple marinades on texture and sensory properties of wild boar and deer longissimus muscle,” DOI: 10.1016/j.meatsci.2012.06.020, PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22766562/ . Pineapple caused the greatest tissue structural changes **but the worst overall sensory appeal** among treatments; kefir delivered high tenderness, best juiciness and overall appeal in that experiment. This was deer/wild-boar muscle **not Maui axis deer**, and findings are preparation-specific.

**2022 original experiment:** “Improving deer meat palatability by treatment with ginger and fermented foods: A deer meat heating study,” https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1878450X22001123 . Deer pieces were marinated **12 hours at 4°C** in specified treatments: **shio-koji and miso reduced cooking loss and rupture stress, and performed well in tenderness/aroma/taste preference**; pineapple and kiwi strongly broke down protein and increased cooking losses. Result applies to the tested process, **not a universal guaranteed 12-hour marinade setting** for any thickness or pineapple preparation.

**Practical implication:** optimize **controlled tenderization + flavor + moisture retention**, not maximal enzymatic proteolysis. Active fresh pineapple varies by cultivar, ripeness, temperature, processing and concentration; heated/canned pineapple often reduces enzyme activity, but this must not be assumed fully inactive without testing. Pineapple flavor and pineapple enzymatic tenderization are separate specifications. Lactose/allergen labeling for kefir and soybean/wheat labeling for miso/shio-koji depend on actual formulations.

## A2. Culinary prototypes — each formulated for 1 lb / 454 g inspected venison
**All measurements here are kitchen-development starting points only.** Use **inspected, traceable, refrigerated** meat; do not use condemned deer or untreated animal blood. These are **not** scientifically validated shelf-life, softening, HACCP or mass-production instructions. For commercial offerings, consult the inspected processor, Hawaiʻi DOH/USDA FSIS as applicable, and validation specialists before sales.

### Prototype 1 — Maui pineapple al pastor (tacos / rice bowls / layered trompo research)
**Use:** thin-sliced, food-grade venison leg or shoulder; 1 lb.
**Marinade:** 1 tbsp achiote paste; 2 dried softened guajillo chiles or 2 tbsp guajillo paste; 2 tbsp orange juice; 1 tbsp lime juice or vinegar; 2 cloves garlic; 1 tsp oregano; 1/2 tsp cumin; salt as appropriate; 1–2 tbsp **pineapple purée**; 1 tbsp neutral oil. Blend smooth, coat evenly, and marinate **refrigerated**.

**Trial design:** baseline with heated pineapple flavoring (reduced bromelain), compared with a *short-exposure, small-dose active pineapple treatment*, rather than committing all product to an unverified overnight fresh-pineapple soak. A **20–30 min cold trial** can be used as an initial culinary comparison only—not a scientifically determined optimum or microbiological safety step. Thin slicing and modest added fat are the main texture safeguards. Use fresh cooked pineapple as garnish.

**Trompo caution:** raw stacked slices and especially any reconstructed **ground/formed trimming cone** have product-specific heating, holding and inspection issues. Browning outside does not prove internal safety. **Do not market a commercial stacked cone without an approved validated process.** Use a hot skillet or controlled test kitchen cooking in small, fully cooked batches for recipe trials.

### Prototype 2 — Shio-koji garlic-ginger venison (most promising balanced texture research)
**Use:** 1 lb medallions, strips, or whole slices.
**Marinade:** 2 tbsp labeled commercial shio-koji; 1 tsp grated ginger; 1 crushed garlic clove; 1 tsp sesame or neutral oil; optional 1 tsp lime; add salt only after tasting because shio-koji is salty. Coat and refrigerate.

**Trial:** test 4, 8 and 12-hour refrigerated treatments using identical cut/thickness and products, including untreated control. The **12-hour, 4°C precedent** is a study condition, not a validated optimal or maximum for local axis deer. Compare texture, loss on cooking, salinity and liking. Serve with quinoa-rice pilaf or in rice bowls.

**Allergens:** shio-koji is fermented rice and generally wheat-free by ingredient concept, but commercial labels vary; verify each supplier's ingredients and cross-contact claims.

### Prototype 3 — Miso-butter venison medallions (umami & moisture)
**Use:** 1 lb sliced venison medallions or strips.
**Marinade:** 1.5 tbsp mild white miso; 1 tsp grated ginger; 1 tsp rice vinegar; 1 tbsp water; 1 tsp oil. Mix; coat; refrigerate in test batches for **4, 8 or 12 hours**, comparing control and objective moisture loss. Cooking butter is **separate**: finish using up to 1 tsp per serving, with portion tracking in nutrition claims.

**Allergens:** miso often contains soy; some types also contain barley/wheat. Confirm formula. Avoid assuming a soy ingredient's allergen declaration is optional.

**Cost path:** study affordable trim, but only if inspected and correctly designated; trim may already be used/sold in ground venison and isn't automatically 'waste' or discounted.

### Prototype 4 — Kefir garlic-herb venison (juicy, mild and approachable)
**Use:** 1 lb medallions/strips.
**Marinade:** 1/2 cup plain **pasteurized commercially produced kefir**; 1 garlic clove; 1 tsp rosemary or oregano; black pepper; a pinch of salt; optional 1 tsp lemon zest. Coat and refrigerate, initially testing **4, 8 and 12 hours** against non-marinated and miso controls. The 2012 study used four days, but that is not a blanket recommendation for home or commercial raw-meat handling.

**Dairy allergen** and cold-chain compliance are mandatory. Do not re-use the raw-meat marinade as a cold serving sauce. A clean extra portion can be reserved before contact with raw meat.

### Prototype 5 — Ready-to-freeze venison rice-pilaf bowls (household and foodservice option)
Cook suitably inspected venison using an approved temperature/process, pair with fully cooked quinoa and rice pilaf, cool rapidly in shallow containers and portion. Freeze what won't be eaten within 3–4 days. Serve with a separately packed pineapple-chile or miso sauce. Reheat cooked leftovers to **165°F**, checking with a thermometer. Protein and calorie values require actual weighed recipes; lean meat does not guarantee low-calorie finished product.

### Prototype 6 — Venison–mycoprotein al pastor burger / formed taco filling (R&D only)
Blend an inspected cooked venison component with a separately produced, **legally qualified food-grade fungal protein** and an appropriate fat/moisture system. Do not assume fungal protein is cheaper than venison or lawful for every regulated meat-product formulation. Quantify cost/serving and blind acceptability versus 100% venison and existing bean/grain alternatives. Food containing venison is **neither vegan nor vegetarian**. Added myoglobin is **not a presumed benefit** because venison already contains muscle myoglobin; a separately authorized synthetic heme product for *plant-based* analogues isn't automatically authorized in actual venison.

## A3. Food safety / freezing / packaging
- Use only **legally inspected food meat**. Keep raw meat and marinade **refrigerated at or below 40°F (4.4°C)**; never marinate unrefrigerated or regard acid, fermentation, kefir or bromelain as sterilization.
- Ground/restructured venison: conservative consumer-safety target **160°F (71°C)** throughout. For whole venison cuts, applicable species/product-specific public-health and FSIS instructions must be checked; the general USDA **145°F plus 3-minute rest** chart applies explicitly to beef/pork/lamb/veal whole cuts and should not be overextended as direct venison-specific regulatory approval. A commercial operation must validate species- and process-specific safe cooking and inspected handling.
- Reheat fully cooked leftovers to **165°F (74°C)**. Promptly refrigerate or freeze; cooked leftovers generally 3–4 days refrigerated. For cooked rice, rapid cooling and cold storage are critical because of *Bacillus cereus* risk.
- Do **not** freeze raw meat in an active bromelain marinade for indefinite time then claim stable tenderness after thawing; proteolysis may resume while thawing. Validate enzyme deactivation, time-temperature and frozen storage texture.
- Clean and sanitized packaging, correct date/lot labeling, allergen disclosure (soy, milk, wheat as applicable), and real pathogen/shelf-life validation are essential for retail or commercial distribution.
- FSIS: https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/steps-keep-food-safe ; https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/grilling-and-food-safety ; https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety .

## A4. Minimum viable palatability experiment
Use a **common inspected venison lot**, matched cut/age/thickness, random-coded treatments: (0) untreated controls; (1) heated pineapple al pastor; (2) limited active-pineapple al pastor; (3) shio-koji; (4) miso; (5) kefir. Equal final safe cooking treatment by product type. Record **marinade uptake %, pH, cut thickness, treatment time/temp, cooking loss %, instrumental shear-force if available, sensory tenderness, juiciness, perceived gaminess, overall flavor and blind repeat-purchase intent**. Replicate across at least two distinct lots before declaring winner. This is an **experimental design recommendation**, not an already completed tasting.

**Go/no-go product acceptance:** stronger blind overall liking than untreated baseline, controllable food safety and shelf life, source-lot traceability, realistic wholesale supply and unit economics, and no invalid 'local origin' claim for imported achiote, miso, kefir or chiles.

## A5. ORCS circular feedstock accounting
Local **Maui pineapple recovery** may supply aromatic juice or peel ingredients, but total recoverable food-grade bromelain quantity and price are **UNKNOWN**. A processor must establish source-segregated, suitable food-grade fractions, pathogen/allergen quality controls and stabilization. Do not claim that retail food-grade pineapple and processing scraps are interchangeable.

Site-wide avoided disposal payments, landowner deer-control incentives, and reduced imported meat must remain distinct lines with **zero realized credit** pending receipts. Public recipes are *open invitations to test*, not ownership claims over established culinary methods.
