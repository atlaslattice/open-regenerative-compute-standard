## Core distinction
An existing PV rooftop upgraded with vegetation has two separate electrical effects: PV generation uplift (verified Australian summer pilot, not US annual) and building HVAC savings. It also carries stormwater, biodiversity, urban heat, waterproof-membrane longevity, aesthetics, worker quality of life, and other non-kWh benefits, plus watering/maintenance/structural loading burdens. Multi-criteria economics can favor an upgrade that looks expensive if judged ONLY by incremental PV kWh.

## 1 m² retrofit energy basis
Assume 180 W PV nameplate/m² gross relevant roof and 1,450 kWh/kW/year yield => existing **261 kWh/m²/year** and 11.1% hypothetical **28.971 kWh/m²/year** incremental PV. Convert 1 m²=10.7639104 square feet. For 1 TWh/year extra, area=1e9/28.971=34.52 million m²=371.6 million square feet. At $2/$5/$10/$15 per sq ft increment CAPEX **$0.74B/$1.86B/$3.72B/$5.57B** per additional TWh of annual PV output. Costs much higher if full green roof must be installed at historic $10+/ft² rather than an economical engineered thin/light vegetation retrofit.

Annual incremental capex charge at 7% real discount, 25yr lifetime, CRF 0.08581 => **$63.8/$159.4/$318.9/$478.3 million/year per TWh/y** = **$63.8/$159.4/$318.9/$478.3 per MWh of incremental solar electricity**, absent operations/maintenance and PV degradation. IMPORTANT corrected math: 1 TWh =1,000,000 MWh, not 1,000,000,000 MWh. Costs per MWh only useful electric increment, NOT total original solar production.

## Ground-mounted facilities and QOL
User explicitly includes small-scale rooftops because greening / cooling/beautification contributes health and QOL even if PV boost modest. Agree socially and treat full 93TWh as *screening universe*, not 93TWh physically eligible. Ground-mounted solar sites use agrovoltaics/pollinator vegetation rather than literal roof green-roof systems; effects of ground cover on bifacial albedo, shading, irrigation and soil health differ, and may reduce PV output in some layouts. Roof safety/stormwater maintenance depend climate; put independent utility values and battery dispatch on ledger.

## Two network architectures
**Jupiter**: central hyperscale training/data facility high fixed-capital, high shared cooling/IT capacity; value mostly derived from training new models or selling centralized inference. Pros scale and expertise; cons grid bottlenecks, long lead times, central failure concentration, water/energy politics, long-haul data movements.
**Lattice**: each factory, LNG terminal, PV site, WWTP, farm, grid battery cluster, desal node has right-sized inference and local data storage, optional replicated regional models and local renewable/thermal assets. Pros latency, fewer WAN raw bytes, process specific optimization, OT resiliency, measurable plant-level returns, small failure domains; cons distributed operation overhead, security patching/management, capex per node, consistency and duplicative compute, site-specific benchmarking. The architectures are complementary: central training upgrades models distributed at industrial edge.
Metrics for comparable ROI include net annual monetary benefits (measured process kWh avoided, quality/yield, lost time prevented, dispatch/power price, water/mineral byproducts, storage arbitrage, network fee avoided), annualized capex+IT power/cooling+cyber costs, avoided downtime and contractual dispatchable capacity. Avoid attributing MIIT factory-wide efficiency increase solely to inference; MIIT China 230 selected excellence factories average +22.3% production efficiency/−20.4% emissions/−50.2% defects, not energy use directly attributable to LLMs.
**Model first 100 MW of distributed industrial IT** on 100 1-MW nodes vs one 100-MW hyperscale inference center, matched total tokens/tasks and uptime. Note central training not an equivalent service; compare delivered useful tasks or economic output rather than training vs inference raw FLOPS.
